# Introduction

Office buildings use most of their water for toilet flushing, cooling towers and landscape irrigation. Two of these three uses do not need drinking-quality water, which makes rainwater an obvious substitute. Design guides give simple estimates of how much mains water a rainwater system will save, usually from annual rainfall, roof area and a fixed collection efficiency. Few of these estimates have been checked against metered use in occupied buildings over several years.

This study metered eight buildings from January 2023 to December 2025. Each had a rainwater tank installed during a refurbishment between 2020 and 2022, feeding toilets in all buildings and irrigation in five. The aim was to compare design estimates with metered savings, and to find which building features explain the differences.

# Buildings and method

## Study buildings

The buildings were chosen from a regional landlord's portfolio to span a range of roof sizes and occupancy. All are four to seven storeys, built between 1978 and 2004, with flat roofs and internal downpipes that could be redirected to a basement tank.

## Metering

Each building had sub-meters on the mains supply, on the rainwater supply to toilets, and on irrigation where present. Pump electricity was metered separately. Readings were logged hourly and checked monthly against the main utility meter. Rainfall came from the nearest weather station, which was within 4 km of every building.

The substitution rate is the share of non-potable demand met by rainwater over a year. The capture rate is the rainwater used divided by the rain falling on the connected roof area.

# Results

## Mains water replaced

Across the three years, rainwater met between 18 and 31 percent of total water use. The spread followed roof area per occupant more closely than any other single feature: buildings with more roof per person could meet a larger share of their toilet demand from rain.

Every building fell short of its design estimate, by 4 to 7 percentage points. The shortfall came from two sources: overflow during heavy rain, when tanks were already full, and dry spells in summer, when tanks emptied and toilets switched back to mains water.

## Tank size

Capture rate rose with tank volume per square metre of roof up to about 25 litres per square metre and then levelled off. B7, with the largest tank relative to its roof, captured 76 percent of the rain, only two points more than B5 with a tank a third smaller per square metre. Above this size, a larger tank mostly stores water through wet months when it would be used anyway.

## Operating cost

Design estimates assumed pump electricity of 0.3 kWh per cubic metre and one filter change a year. Metered pump use averaged 0.55 kWh per cubic metre, mainly because pumps cycled on and off for small demands at night. Filters in the five buildings with irrigation needed changing two to three times a year because of leaf debris from nearby trees.

# Discussion

The gap between design and metered savings was consistent enough to correct for. Reducing design estimates by about a fifth would have predicted every building within 3 percentage points. The operating cost findings matter more for payback than the water savings: with the metered pump energy and filter changes, payback lengthened from the planned 9 years to between 11 and 16 years at current water prices.

Two changes would close part of that gap at low cost. A small pressure vessel on the toilet supply would stop pumps cycling for single flushes at night, and a leaf screen on downpipes would cut filter changes in buildings near trees. Both were tested in B6 during 2025 and reduced pump energy by about a quarter.

# Conclusions

Rainwater reuse in mid-size offices replaced a fifth to a third of mains water in this study, less than design guides predict but a stable share from year to year. Roof area per occupant sets the ceiling, and tanks larger than about 25 litres per square metre of roof add little. Operating costs, not water savings, decide the payback, and they deserve the same attention at design stage.
